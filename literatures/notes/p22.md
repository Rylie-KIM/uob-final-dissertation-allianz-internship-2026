## 🇰🇷 한국어 요약

### 1. 문제 정의 — 지도 학습에서의 기회 평등

머신러닝 모델이 보호 속성(인종, 성별 등)에 의해 차별적인 결정을 내리지 않도록 보장하는 기준을 어떻게 공식화할 것인가?

기존의 두 가지 접근은 모두 결함이 있다:

- **인구통계학적 동등성(Demographic Parity):** $P(\hat{Y}=1 \mid A=0) = P(\hat{Y}=1 \mid A=1)$ 을 요구. 하지만 집단별 기저율(base rate)이 다를 때 이는 완벽한 예측 $\hat{Y}=Y$ 자체를 금지함 — 즉 정확도와 공정성이 근본적으로 충돌.
- **공정성 통한 무지(Fairness through Unawareness):** 보호 속성 A를 모델 입력에서 제외. 하지만 A와 상관된 다른 특징들이 A의 대리자(redundant encoding)로 기능하기 때문에 실질적 효과 없음.

논문의 핵심 통찰: **진정한 비차별 기준은 예측 결과 $\hat{Y}$가 보호 속성 A와 결과 Y 모두에 대해 어떻게 관련되어 있는지를 조건부로 평가해야 한다.**

보험 사기 탐지에서의 직접 번역: SFP 루프는 모델 v1이 특정 청구 유형(예: 특정 우편번호, 고령 운전자)을 집중 조사하게 하고, 그 결과 v2 학습 데이터에서 해당 집단의 사기 라벨이 과대표되어 집단별 TPR/FPR이 체계적으로 왜곡된다.

### 2. 핵심 기술 개념 및 수식

#### 2-1. 균등화된 기회 (Equalized Odds) — 강한 정의

**Definition 2.1:** 예측기 $\hat{Y}$가 보호 속성 A와 결과 Y에 대해 *equalized odds*를 만족한다는 것은 $\hat{Y}$와 A가 Y를 조건으로 독립임을 의미:

$$\hat{Y} \perp A \mid Y$$

이진 분류의 경우 이는 다음 두 조건을 동시에 요구:

$$P\{\hat{Y}=1 \mid A=0, Y=y\} = P\{\hat{Y}=1 \mid A=1, Y=y\}, \quad y \in \{0, 1\}$$

- $y=1$일 때: **집단 간 진양성률(TPR) 동일** 요구
- $y=0$일 때: **집단 간 위양성률(FPR) 동일** 요구

완벽한 예측 $\hat{Y}=Y$는 자동으로 equalized odds를 만족 — 인구통계학적 동등성과의 근본 차이.

#### 2-2. 기회 평등 (Equal Opportunity) — 약한 정의

**Definition 2.2:** $\hat{Y}$가 *equal opportunity*를 만족한다는 것은 "유리한 결과(Y=1)" 집단에서만 비차별을 요구:

$$P\{\hat{Y}=1 \mid A=0, Y=1\} = P\{\hat{Y}=1 \mid A=1, Y=1\}$$

즉 **집단 간 TPR만 동일**하면 됨. FPR에는 제약 없음. Equalized odds보다 약하지만 실용적으로 더 달성하기 쉽고 효용 손실이 적음.

보험 사기 맥락: Y=1을 "실제 사기"로 해석하면, 실제 사기 청구자가 어느 집단에 속하든 동일한 확률로 모델에 의해 의심 대상으로 지목되어야 한다는 요구.

#### 2-3. 사후 보정 알고리즘 (Post-Processing)

모델 R(점수 함수)이 주어졌을 때, R과 A만의 함수로서 파생 예측기 $\widetilde{Y}$를 구성:

**핵심 구조 (각 집단 $a \in \{0,1\}$에 대해):**

$$\widetilde{Y}|(A=a) = \mathbb{I}\{R > T_a\}$$

여기서 $T_a$는 집단별로 다른 임계값 (possibly randomized). 구체적으로 $T_a$는 확률 $\bar{p}_a$로 상위 임계값 $\bar{t}_a$, 확률 $1-\bar{p}_a$로 하위 임계값 $\underline{t}_a$를 취하는 혼합 임계값.

**최적화 문제 (4.3):**

$$\min_{\widetilde{Y}} \mathbb{E}\ell(\widetilde{Y}, Y) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \forall a \in \{0,1\}: \gamma_a(\widetilde{Y}) \in P_a(\widehat{Y}), \quad \gamma_0(\widetilde{Y}) = \gamma_1(\widetilde{Y})$$

여기서 $\gamma_a(\widehat{Y}) \overset{\text{def}}{=} \left(\Pr\{\widehat{Y}=1 \mid A=a, Y=0\},\ \Pr\{\widehat{Y}=1 \mid A=a, Y=1\}\right)$이고, $P_a(\widehat{Y})$는 파생 예측기들이 달성 가능한 (FPR, TPR) 공간의 볼록 껍질.

**Proposition 4.4:** 이 최적화 문제는 4개 변수의 선형 계획(LP)이며, $(\widetilde{Y}, A, Y)$의 결합 분포로부터 계수를 계산할 수 있다.

#### 2-4. Bayes 최적성 (Section 5)

**Proposition 5.2:** Bayes 최적 회귀기 $R(X,A)$와 임의의 비차별 기준 C에 대해, C를 만족하는 최적 예측기 $Y^*$가 항상 존재하며 이는 $(R,A)$만의 함수로 파생될 수 있다.

**근-최적성 (Theorem 5.6):** 임의의 점수 함수 $\hat{R}$에 대해:

$$\mathbb{E}\ell(\widetilde{Y}, Y) \leq \mathbb{E}\ell(Y^*, Y) + 2\sqrt{2} \cdot d_K(\hat{R}, R^*)$$

여기서 $d_K$는 조건부 Kolmogorov 거리. **Bayes 최적 회귀기에 가까울수록 equalized odds 달성 비용이 줄어든다.**

#### 2-5. 단일불가식별성 (Section 6)

**Proposition 6.1:** 두 가지 서로 다른 의존 구조(Scenario I: A → X₁ → R*, Scenario II: X₃ → Y, X₃ corr A)가 $(Y, A, R, \widetilde{R})$의 동일한 결합 분포를 생성할 수 있다. **어떤 oblivious 테스트(점수+보호속성+결과만 보는)도 두 시나리오를 구분할 수 없다.**

이는 "equalized odds 검사 통과 = 진정한 공정성"이 성립하지 않음을 의미 — 인과적 구조를 모르면 한계가 있음.

```python
def check_equalized_odds(self, df, score_col, label_col, group_col, threshold=0.5):
    """
    P22 Lemma 4.2 기반: gamma_a(Y_hat) 계산 및 집단 간 비교
    Returns: dict with TPR_gap, FPR_gap per group pair
    """
    results = {}
    groups = df[group_col].unique()
    for a0, a1 in combinations(groups, 2):
        mask0 = df[group_col] == a0
        mask1 = df[group_col] == a1
        
        # TPR: P(Y_hat=1 | A=a, Y=1)
        tpr0 = (df[mask0 & (df[label_col]==1)][score_col] > threshold).mean()
        tpr1 = (df[mask1 & (df[label_col]==1)][score_col] > threshold).mean()
        
        # FPR: P(Y_hat=1 | A=a, Y=0)
        fpr0 = (df[mask0 & (df[label_col]==0)][score_col] > threshold).mean()
        fpr1 = (df[mask1 & (df[label_col]==0)][score_col] > threshold).mean()
        
        results[(a0, a1)] = {
            'TPR_gap': abs(tpr0 - tpr1),
            'FPR_gap': abs(fpr0 - fpr1),
            'equal_opportunity_violation': abs(tpr0 - tpr1) > 0.05
        }
    return results
```

## 🇬🇧 English Summary

### 1. Problem — Non-discrimination in Supervised Learning

How do we formally ensure that an ML classifier does not discriminate against protected attributes (race, gender, etc.) while still being useful?

Two naive approaches both fail:

- **Demographic parity:** $P(\hat{Y}=1 \mid A=0) = P(\hat{Y}=1 \mid A=1)$. This is flawed because it forbids the perfect predictor $\hat{Y}=Y$ when base rates differ across groups — accuracy and parity are then fundamentally at odds.
- **Fairness through unawareness:** Remove A from model inputs. Fails because redundant encodings allow other features to act as proxies for A.

The paper's core insight: **a sound non-discrimination criterion must evaluate the prediction $\hat{Y}$ conditional on both A and the true outcome Y.** This naturally aligns fairness with the goal of building accurate predictors.

In Allianz's setting: the SFP loop drives model v1 to over-investigate certain claim segments (e.g., specific postcodes, claim types), causing disproportionate fraud labels in those segments. Retraining on this biased data systematically distorts group-level TPR/FPR in v2 and v3.

### 2. Core Technical Concepts and Formulae

#### 2-1. Equalized Odds — The Stronger Criterion

**Definition 2.1:** A predictor $\hat{Y}$ satisfies *equalized odds* with respect to protected attribute A and outcome Y if $\hat{Y}$ and A are independent conditional on Y:

$$\hat{Y} \perp A \mid Y$$

In the binary case this requires both:

$$P\{\hat{Y}=1 \mid A=0, Y=y\} = P\{\hat{Y}=1 \mid A=1, Y=y\}, \quad y \in \{0, 1\}$$

- For $y=1$: **equal true positive rates** across groups
- For $y=0$: **equal false positive rates** across groups

The perfect predictor $\hat{Y}=Y$ automatically satisfies this — the key distinction from demographic parity.

#### 2-2. Equal Opportunity — The Weaker Criterion

**Definition 2.2:** $\hat{Y}$ satisfies *equal opportunity* if non-discrimination is required only within the "advantaged" outcome group ($Y=1$):

$$P\{\hat{Y}=1 \mid A=0, Y=1\} = P\{\hat{Y}=1 \mid A=1, Y=1\}$$

This requires only **equal TPR** across groups; FPR is unconstrained. Weaker than equalized odds but achieves higher utility in practice.

In insurance fraud: if $Y=1$ means "genuinely fraudulent claim", then all fraudulent claimants should have an equal probability of being flagged regardless of group membership.

#### 2-3. Post-Processing Algorithm (Section 4)

Given a learned score $R$ and protected attribute A, construct a derived predictor $\widetilde{Y}$ that is a function only of $(R, A)$:

For each group $a \in \{0,1\}$, the predictor uses a (possibly randomised) group-specific threshold $T_a$ taking value $\underline{t}_a$ with probability $\bar{p}_a$ and $\bar{t}_a$ with probability $1-\bar{p}_a$.

**Optimisation problem (4.3):**

$$\min_{\widetilde{Y}} \mathbb{E}\ell(\widetilde{Y}, Y) \quad \text{s.t.} \quad \forall a: \gamma_a(\widetilde{Y}) \in P_a(\widehat{Y}), \quad \gamma_0(\widetilde{Y}) = \gamma_1(\widetilde{Y})$$

where $\gamma_a(\widehat{Y}) = \left(\Pr\{\widehat{Y}=1 \mid A=a, Y=0\},\ \Pr\{\widehat{Y}=1 \mid A=a, Y=1\}\right)$ and $P_a(\widehat{Y})$ is the convex hull of achievable (FPR, TPR) pairs.

**Proposition 4.4:** This is a linear program in four variables, efficiently solvable from the joint distribution of $(\widetilde{Y}, A, Y)$.

#### 2-4. Bayes Optimality (Section 5)

**Proposition 5.2:** For any source distribution over $(Y, X, A)$ with Bayes optimal regressor $R(X,A)$, for any loss function and any oblivious non-discrimination criterion C, there exists a predictor $Y^*(R,A)$ satisfying C that is optimal.

**Near-optimality (Theorem 5.6):** For any score $\hat{R}$:

$$\mathbb{E}\ell(\widetilde{Y}, Y) \leq \mathbb{E}\ell(Y^*, Y) + 2\sqrt{2} \cdot d_K(\hat{R}, R^*)$$

The closer the learned regressor is to Bayes optimal, the smaller the cost of imposing equalized odds.

#### 2-5. Non-Identifiability (Section 6)

**Proposition 6.1:** Two fundamentally different causal structures (discriminatory vs. non-discriminatory) can produce **identical joint distributions** of $(Y, A, R, \widetilde{R})$. No oblivious test (observing only score + protected attribute + outcome) can distinguish the two scenarios.

This means that passing an equalized odds audit does not guarantee true causal fairness — causal structure matters.

### 3. Experimental Results — FICO Credit Score Case Study

Real FICO credit score data across racial groups (Black, Hispanic, White, Asian):

| Condition | Result |
|-----------|--------|
| Original FICO (single threshold) | Black/Hispanic groups have lower TPR and higher FPR than White/Asian |
| After equal opportunity correction | Equal TPR achieved; White/Asian acceptance rates slightly decrease, Black/Hispanic slightly increase |
| After equalized odds correction | Both TPR and FPR equalised; larger accuracy loss, but equal error rates across all groups |

**Key finding:** A single-threshold policy structurally generates discrimination when group score distributions differ. Post-processing achieves substantial fairness improvement at modest utility cost.

#### 4-2. Why SFP Loops Break Equalized Odds

SFP loop mechanics through the equalized odds lens:

1. Model v1 has high investigation rate for `postcode_risk = high` → over-samples fraud labels from this group
2. Training data for v2 has inflated $P(Y=1 \mid A=\text{high\_risk})$
3. v2 learns a higher score for high-risk postcodes, achieving inflated TPR for that group
4. For low-risk postcodes, insufficient investigation means fraud goes undetected → artificially low TPR
5. v3 reinforces this: $\text{TPR}_{A=\text{high}} \gg \text{TPR}_{A=\text{low}}$ — equalized odds violated

**This is the SFP loop as a fairness failure, not just an accuracy failure.**

### 5. Limitations

- **Oblivious limitation:** Section 6's non-identifiability result — satisfying equalized odds does not guarantee causal fairness. The same joint distribution can arise from discriminatory and non-discriminatory mechanisms.
- **Randomisation requirement:** When group-level ROC curves do not intersect, achieving equalized odds requires randomised thresholds — operationally complex in insurance triage.
- **Post-processing data requirements:** Proposition 4.4's LP requires the joint distribution of $(\widetilde{Y}, A, Y)$ — in Allianz's context, labels exist only for investigated claims, compounding P1's selective labels problem.
- **Base rate differences:** Forcing equalized odds when true fraud rates differ across groups may increase false positives in lower-prevalence groups.

Model v1 over-investigates high-risk postcodes, so fraud labels pile up there. v2 learns higher scores for that segment, raising its TPR artificially. Low-risk postcode claimants who are actually fraudulent get missed — low TPR. By Hardt et al.'s definition, $\hat{Y} \perp A \mid Y$ is violated. The model doesn't just perform worse overall — it fails specific groups more than others, and that gap compounds with each model version." |
| *"What's the difference between demographic parity and equalized odds?"* | "Demographic parity requires equal acceptance rates regardless of the true outcome. Equalized odds — from Hardt et al. (NeurIPS 2016) — requires equal TPR *and* equal FPR conditional on the true label. The key advantage: the perfect predictor $\hat{Y}=Y$ automatically satisfies equalized odds but violates demographic parity when base rates differ. For fraud detection where fraud rates genuinely vary by claim type, demographic parity is the wrong target." |
| *"Can we fix an existing model for fairness without retraining?"* | "Yes — that's exactly what Hardt et al. propose. Given any learned score and the protected attribute, you solve a linear program over the convex hull of achievable (FPR, TPR) pairs for each group, then apply group-specific thresholds. Proposition 4.4 proves this LP is efficient to solve. For Allianz, this means we can post-process model_v3_score with group-specific thresholds by postcode risk band or claim type, satisfying equalized odds without touching the training pipeline." |
| *"Is equalized odds enough to prove the model is fair?"* | "Not causally — that's Proposition 6.1's non-identifiability result. Two fundamentally different causal structures (one discriminatory, one not) produce identical joint distributions of score, protected attribute, and outcome. So passing an equalized odds audit doesn't rule out discriminatory mechanisms. For a complete fairness argument you'd combine equalized odds measurement (Build 02) with causal analysis (Build 06 via DoWhy)." |
| *"How does Build 05's randomisation strategy interact with equalized odds?"* | "Randomisation generates new labels in previously under-investigated segments. P22 tells us which segments are most fairness-critical — those with the worst equalized odds violations. So the exploration budget in ε-greedy or Thompson sampling should be skewed toward those segments. The loop breaks not just because we collect more data, but because we target our uncertainty reduction where equalized odds needs it most." |
| *"What would you tell an FCA/Consumer Duty reviewer about the model's fairness?"* | "I'd show group-level TPR/FPR breakdowns using P22's $\gamma_a$ framework — this is exactly the kind of oblivious, interpretable measure regulators can audit without seeing the full model internals. I'd also show that after applying the post-processing correction, equalized odds gaps fall below a specified tolerance. The FCA's 'fair outcomes' principle maps directly to equal opportunity at minimum: every claimant who is genuinely committing fraud should have an equal chance of being flagged, regardless of their postcode or claim history." |
| *"How does this paper connect to the impossibility results in p23/p24?"* | "Hardt et al. show equalized odds is achievable via post-processing and aligns with Bayes optimality. But Chouldechova (2017, referenced in p23) and Corbett-Davies et al. (p24) prove you cannot simultaneously satisfy equalized odds (equal FPR + equal TPR) and calibration when base rates differ. So P22's solution is internally consistent but exists in tension with other fairness criteria — the choice between them depends on which error type is costlier for Allianz." |
| *"What's the near-optimality guarantee of the post-processing approach?"* | "Theorem 5.6 bounds the additional loss from imposing equalized odds: it's at most $2\sqrt{2} \cdot d_K(\hat{R}, R^*)$ where $d_K$ is the conditional Kolmogorov distance between our score and the Bayes optimal. So the better our fraud model is in the first place, the cheaper equalized odds becomes. This argues for investing in model quality (Build 01 simulation, Build 06 causal debias) as a prerequisite to fairness correction." |
