## 🇰🇷 한국어 요약

### 1. 문제 정의 — "머신러닝 루프"와 공정성

이 책은 단일 논문이 아닌 9개 챕터로 구성된 교과서로, 머신러닝 기반 자동 의사결정의 공정성 문제를 **측정 → 학습 → 행동 → 피드백**의 순환 구조로 분석한다.

핵심 진단 (Chapter 1 Figure 1.1, "The Machine Learning Loop"):

```
상태(State of the World) <---- (피드백) ---- 개인(Individuals)
        |                                          ^
   측정(Measurement)                         행동(Action)
        |                                          |
       데이터(Data) ----(학습/Learning)----> 모델(Model)
```

이 루프의 핵심 문제: **모델의 행동이 세계의 상태를 바꾸고, 그 바뀐 상태에서 다시 학습이 이루어진다**. 공정성 문제는 이 루프의 각 단계에서 누적된다:

- **측정 단계:** 대상 변수(Y)의 정의 자체가 편향될 수 있음 (예: 체포 기록을 범죄의 대리 변수로 사용 → 경찰 활동이 편향된 경우 라벨 자체가 편향)
- **데이터 단계:** 역사적 불평등이 훈련 데이터에 반영됨
- **모델 단계:** 보정된(calibrated) 모델은 데이터의 집단별 기저율 차이를 그대로 재현함
- **행동 단계:** 모델 출력이 의사결정에 사용되면 집단별로 차별적 영향 발생
- **피드백 단계:** 피드백이 편향되면 루프가 강화됨 → SFP(자기실현적 예언)

### 2. 핵심 기술 개념 및 수식

#### 2-1. 세 가지 공정성 기준의 구조 (Chapter 3)

책은 공정성 기준을 크게 세 가지로 분류:

**독립성 (Independence = 인구통계학적 동등성):**
$$\hat{Y} \perp A$$

집단 간 결정 비율 동일 요구. 기저율이 다를 때 정확한 예측과 근본적으로 충돌.

**분리성 (Separation = Equalized Odds):**
$$\hat{Y} \perp A \mid Y$$

진양성률(TPR)과 위양성률(FPR) 모두 집단 간 동일 요구. P22 (Hardt et al.)의 equalized odds와 동일.

**충분성 (Sufficiency = 보정):**
$$Y \perp A \mid \hat{Y}$$

동일한 점수를 받은 개인은 집단에 관계없이 동일한 실제 결과 확률을 가져야 함. 즉: $P(Y=1 \mid \hat{Y}=s, A=0) = P(Y=1 \mid \hat{Y}=s, A=1)$

#### 2-2. 세 기준의 관계: Chouldechova 불가능성 정리 (Chapter 3)

**핵심 결과:** 집단 간 기저율이 다를 때(즉 $P(Y=1 \mid A=0) \neq P(Y=1 \mid A=1)$), 보정 + 동일 FPR + 동일 FNR을 **동시에 만족하는 이진 예측기는 존재하지 않는다.**

수식으로: 보정된 점수 R에 대해 집단별 양성 예측값(PPV)과 음성 예측값(NPV)을 정의하면:

$$PPV_a = P(Y=1 \mid R=r, A=a) = r \quad \text{(보정 조건)}$$

기저율이 $\pi_a = P(Y=1 \mid A=a)$일 때, FPR과 FNR 사이의 관계:

$$FPR_a = \frac{(1-\pi_a)(1-\text{precision}_a)}{\pi_a \cdot \text{recall}_a} \cdot \frac{\pi_a}{1-\pi_a}$$

$\pi_0 \neq \pi_1$이면 동일한 보정을 유지하면서 FPR과 FNR을 동시에 집단 간 동일화할 수 없음. 셋 중 하나를 희생해야 함.

**Allianz SFP 맥락:** SFP 루프가 고위험 우편번호의 사기 라벨 기저율을 인위적으로 높이면, 보정된 모델은 자동으로 해당 집단에 더 높은 점수를 부여 → 불가능성 정리에 의해 이 집단의 FPR이나 FNR 중 하나가 다른 집단과 달라짐.

#### 2-3. 피드백 루프와 자기실현적 예언 (Chapter 1, Section "Feedback and feedback loops")

책은 세 종류의 피드백을 구별:

**1. 자기실현적 예측 (Self-fulfilling predictions):**
예측 자체가 결과를 야기. 예: 예측적 경찰 활동 시스템이 특정 지역을 고위험으로 분류 → 경찰 집중 배치 → 해당 지역에서 더 많은 체포 → 알고리즘 "검증" (하지만 인과적으로는 잘못된 검증).

**2. 훈련 데이터에 영향을 주는 예측 (Predictions that affect the training set):**
예측에 기반한 행동이 향후 학습 데이터를 형성. Lum & Isaac (2016)이 PredPol 알고리즘 분석: 흑인 지역을 마약 범죄 고위험으로 표시 → 해당 지역 집중 단속 → 더 많은 마약 관련 체포 → 다음 모델이 이를 "실제 위험 신호"로 학습. **이것이 SFP 루프의 교과서적 사례.**

**3. 현상 자체에 영향을 주는 예측 (Predictions that affect the phenomenon):**
모델이 측정하려는 현상 자체를 변화시킴. 장기적 구조 변화.

#### 2-4. 측정 편향 (Chapter 1, "The trouble with measurement")

대상 변수(Y) 정의의 위험성:
- Y = "사기 여부"를 Y = "조사 후 사기로 판명"으로 측정하면, Y 자체가 조사 정책의 함수가 됨
- 이는 P1 (Lakkaraju et al.)의 selective labels 문제와 동일한 메커니즘

식: $Y_{\text{observed}} = Y_{\text{true}} \cdot \mathbb{I}[\text{investigated}]$이므로 $P(Y_{\text{observed}} = 1) < P(Y_{\text{true}} = 1)$ — 미조사 청구건의 실제 사기는 보이지 않음.

#### 2-5. 보정(Calibration)과 오차율 패리티의 규범적 토대 (Chapter 4)

책은 보정이 왜 중요한지를 규범적으로 설명:
- 보정: 점수 r을 받은 모든 개인에게 "당신의 위험은 r%"라는 동일한 의미를 부여
- 오차율 패리티: 시스템이 집단에 상관없이 같은 방식으로 작동한다는 보장

둘 다 가질 수 없는 상황에서의 선택은 가치 판단(규범적 선택)의 문제 — 기술적으로 해결 불가능.

#### 2-6. 인과적 공정성 (Chapter 5)

Chapter 5는 인과적 구분을 형식화:
- **경로별 인과적 효과(Path-specific causal effects):** A → $\hat{Y}$ 경로 중 A → X → $\hat{Y}$ (간접, 허용 가능) vs. A → $\hat{Y}$ (직접, 차별)
- **반사실적 공정성:** 개인이 다른 집단 구성원이었다면 다른 결정을 받았을까?
- **Confounding (교란):** 관찰되지 않은 변수가 A와 Y 모두에 영향 → 인과 추론 없이는 분리 불가

## 🇬🇧 English Summary

### 1. Problem — The Machine Learning Loop

This book is a 9-chapter textbook treating fairness in ML through the lens of a closed-loop sociotechnical system. Its central insight is Figure 1.1: the ML pipeline is not a one-shot prediction exercise but a closed loop — State of the World → Measurement → Data → Learning → Model → Action → (Feedback back to) Individuals → State of the World.

Fairness problems accumulate at every stage:
- **Measurement:** The target variable Y may itself encode bias. If Y = "fraud found by investigators" and investigators are selective, the label mechanism is endogenous.
- **Data:** Historical inequalities are encoded in training data; calibrated models faithfully reproduce them.
- **Action:** Model outputs drive decisions that have disparate group-level effects.
- **Feedback:** Biased feedback reinforces the loop — the self-fulfilling prophecy.

In Allianz's context: fraud labels are produced only for investigated claims; investigations are driven by model scores; model scores are trained on past labels — the ML loop is closed and self-reinforcing.

### 2. Core Technical Concepts and Formulae

#### 2-1. Three Fairness Criteria (Chapter 3)

The book reduces the vast fairness literature to three mutually exclusive statistical criteria:

**Independence (Demographic Parity):** $\hat{Y} \perp A$

**Separation (Equalized Odds):** $\hat{Y} \perp A \mid Y$

**Sufficiency (Calibration):** $Y \perp A \mid \hat{Y}$, i.e., $P(Y=1 \mid \hat{Y}=s, A=0) = P(Y=1 \mid \hat{Y}=s, A=1)$

Each corresponds to a different moral intuition. None is sufficient on its own to certify fairness. Satisfying one may violate another.

#### 2-2. Chouldechova's Impossibility Theorem (Chapter 3)

**Key result:** When base rates differ across groups ($P(Y=1 \mid A=0) \neq P(Y=1 \mid A=1)$), no binary classifier can simultaneously satisfy calibration, equal FPR, and equal FNR.

Formally, for a calibrated score with group-level base rates $\pi_0 \neq \pi_1$:

$$\text{FPR}_a = \frac{(1-\pi_a)(1-\text{PPV}_a)}{\pi_a \cdot \text{TPR}_a} \cdot \frac{\pi_a}{1-\pi_a}$$

If PPV is equal across groups (calibration) and TPR is equal (equal FNR), then FPR must differ when $\pi_0 \neq \pi_1$. The three conditions are mutually exclusive when base rates differ.

This is the theoretical foundation explaining why the ProPublica/Northpointe COMPAS dispute was irresolvable: both sides were correct given their chosen criterion.

#### 2-3. Three Types of Feedback Loops (Chapter 1)

The book distinguishes three feedback mechanisms:

1. **Self-fulfilling predictions:** The prediction causes the outcome. A high fraud score triggers investigation which discovers fraud — validating the score even if the underlying risk was identical across groups.

2. **Predictions that affect the training set:** Actions based on predictions shape future training data. Predictive policing sends more officers to predicted-high-crime areas → more arrests there → next model trained on arrest data learns that area = high crime. This compounds over model versions.

3. **Predictions that affect the phenomenon:** Long-term structural effects on the population the model is meant to model.

#### 2-4. Measurement Bias (Chapter 1)

Target variable contamination: when Y is measured as "outcome observed conditional on intervention", labels are endogenous to the decision process. For fraud detection:

$$Y_{\text{observed}} = Y_{\text{true}} \cdot \mathbb{I}[\text{investigated}]$$

The observed label rate $P(Y_{\text{observed}}=1)$ is a biased proxy for true fraud rate $P(Y_{\text{true}}=1)$. This is precisely the selective labels problem (P1).

#### 2-5. Causal Fairness (Chapter 5)

Chapter 5 formalises causality for fairness analysis using Pearl's do-calculus framework:
- **Confounding:** Unobserved variables affecting both A and Y — standard observational tests cannot identify causal discrimination
- **Path-specific effects:** Distinguish A → $\hat{Y}$ direct path (discriminatory) from A → X → $\hat{Y}$ path through legitimate features (acceptable)
- **Counterfactual fairness:** Would the individual have received a different decision if they had belonged to a different group?

### 3. Key Examples and Case Studies

#### 3-1. COMPAS and Impossibility in Practice

The COMPAS recidivism score controversy is the book's illustration of Chouldechova's theorem in practice. ProPublica found Black defendants misclassified as high-risk at twice the rate of White defendants (unequal FPR). Northpointe countered that the score was calibrated within each racial group (sufficiency). Both claims are simultaneously true and mathematically consistent with Chouldechova's theorem — when base rates of recidivism differ, calibration and equal FPR cannot coexist. The dispute was a value choice disguised as a technical disagreement.

#### 3-2. Structural Discrimination (Chapter 8)

The Uber gender wage gap case study: the algorithm itself is gender-neutral, but structural factors (preference for night shifts, location choices) produce wage gaps. ML can amplify structural inequalities without being discriminatory in the algorithmic sense. The lesson: algorithmic fairness corrections that leave structural causes untouched may be insufficient.

#### 4-2. Why This Book Is the Theoretical Backbone

Unlike the other papers (which are single-topic), P23 provides the complete conceptual map: it defines the problem (ML loop, Ch. 1), gives the formal criteria (Ch. 3), provides the impossibility result (Ch. 3), explains measurement bias (Ch. 1), introduces causality (Ch. 5), and contextualises structural factors (Ch. 8). Every build phase of the Allianz SFP project can find its theoretical home in one of these chapters.

### 5. Limitations

- **US-centric legal framework:** Authors explicitly acknowledge the book assumes US law and moral tradition. The EU AI Act and FCA Consumer Duty require separate mapping (addressed by P25).
- **Mathematical criteria are insufficient:** Satisfying any fairness criterion does not certify genuine fairness — the non-identifiability results (also in P22) show that different dependency structures can produce identical statistical signatures.
- **Structural causes not addressed:** Algorithmic fixes applied to a structurally unjust system may entrench rather than remedy injustice.
- **Observational data limitations:** Causal identification of discrimination sources requires experiments or strong assumptions beyond what observational claims data alone can support.

| Prompt | Use P23 to say… |
|--------|----------------|
| *"What's your theoretical framework for thinking about SFP loops?"* | "Barocas, Hardt and Narayanan (fairmlbook) give us the most useful framing: the ML pipeline is a closed loop — measurement, learning, action, feedback. In Allianz's case, investigation decisions feed back into training data, which feeds into the next model version. The loop is self-reinforcing. Figure 1.1 in the book is literally a diagram of the problem we're solving." |
| *"Why can't we just use a calibrated fraud model and call it fair?"* | "Chouldechova's impossibility theorem from Chapter 3 of the fairmlbook. If fraud base rates differ across claim types or postcodes — and they do — then a calibrated model inevitably has unequal false positive or false negative rates across groups. Both conditions cannot hold simultaneously. So calibration and equalized error rates are in irresolvable tension; we must choose, and that choice is a value judgment, not a technical one." |
| *"How is the SFP loop different from ordinary model bias?"* | "Chapter 1 distinguishes three feedback types. Ordinary bias is static — it reflects historical inequalities. The SFP loop is dynamic: the model's predictions actively shape the training data for the next model. It's the second type — 'predictions that affect the training set.' Each retraining cycle the bias compounds. The Lum & Isaac PredPol study referenced in the book is the exact analogue of what's happening with Allianz's fraud model versions." |
| *"Is the fraud label a good target variable?"* | "Not without correction. The fairmlbook's 'trouble with measurement' section makes this explicit: if Y is observed only where the model looked, then $Y_{observed} = Y_{true} \cdot \mathbf{1}[investigated]$. The label is endogenous to the investigation policy, which is driven by the model score. That's what Build 00 has to audit before any modelling — and it's the same selective labels problem P1 (Lakkaraju, KDD '17) formalises mathematically." |
| *"What's the difference between separation and sufficiency as fairness criteria?"* | "Both come from Chapter 3. Separation — equalized odds — says the model's error rates should be equal across groups: same TPR, same FPR. Sufficiency — calibration — says the same score should mean the same risk regardless of group membership. The impossibility theorem says you can't have both when base rates differ. For fraud detection I'd argue separation matters more: we want every genuinely fraudulent claimant to have an equal chance of being flagged, not just an equal score interpretation." |
| *"How does Build 06's causal mitigation connect to fairness theory?"* | "Chapter 5 of the fairmlbook introduces path-specific causal effects. When we use DoWhy to estimate the causal effect of being in a high-risk postcode on fraud score, we're separating the legitimate path (postcode → true risk → score) from the discriminatory path (postcode → investigation intensity → label → score). IPW reweighting in Build 06 targets exactly the discriminatory path — it's operationalising Chapter 5's causal fairness framework." |
| *"What are the limits of your proposed framework?"* | "The fairmlbook is honest about this in its preface: mathematical fairness criteria are necessary but not sufficient. You can satisfy equalized odds and still have a system that perpetuates structural inequality — the Uber wage gap chapter illustrates this. For Allianz, the algorithmic fix (Build 06 IPW) might not address structural reasons why certain claim types are investigated more aggressively in the first place. That's a process and governance question beyond what the technical pipeline can solve alone." |
| *"How does this relate to the EU AI Act compliance argument?"* | "The ML loop in Chapter 1 maps directly to Article 15 of the EU AI Act — which requires high-risk AI systems that continue to learn to prevent 'feedback loops' from distorting future outputs.
